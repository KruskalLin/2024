## Data

We consider a **centered** point cloud $X \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times n}$ together with a connected graph $G$ whose nodes are the points and whose edges encode neighborhood or interaction structure. The graph $G$ come from a $r$-radius graph construction.

Our goal is to construct global or local orthonormal frames in $\mathbb{R}^3$ that depend on $(X,G)$ in an $O(3)$-equivariant way. Whenever a construction only yields a finite set of possible frames $\mathcal F(X) \subset O(3)$, we can enforce equivariance by averaging over this finite set
$$
\langle \Phi \rangle_{\mathcal F}(X)
= \frac{1}{|\mathcal F(X)|}\sum_{g \in \mathcal F(X)} g \cdot \Phi(g^{-1}\cdot X),
$$
where $\Phi$ is a non-equivariant base model that consumes coordinates expressed in a chosen frame. The continuity and stability of $\mathcal F(X)$ with respect to small changes in $X$ are crucial for making this averaging practical.

## Global Frame I: PCA-Based Frame Averaging

In the PCA-based approach, we construct a covariance matrix
$$
C = XX^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}.
$$
We then take its eigendecomposition
$$
C = Q \Lambda Q^\top,
$$
with $Q \in O(3)$ orthogonal and $\Lambda$ diagonal with non-negative eigenvalues. The columns of $Q$ are principal directions of variance in the point cloud. Because flipping the sign of any eigenvector leaves both $C$ and the orthogonality of $Q$ unchanged, there are $2^3 = 8$ distinct sign configurations that all represent valid orthonormal frames compatible with $C$. Thus, the frame set $\mathcal F(X)$ naturally includes all eight sign variants of $Q$, and we typically use frame averaging over these possibilities.

The main difficulty arises in **degenerate or near-degenerate cases of the spectrum**. If **some eigenvalues are very small or zero**, the point cloud is close to being planar or even linear. A zero eigenvalue means that all points lie in a plane (rank 2) or a line (rank 1), so the corresponding eigenvector is only defined up to any rotation in the null space. If **eigenvalues are repeated**, the eigenspace has dimension greater than one, and any orthonormal basis within that eigenspace is acceptable. These cases lead to infinitely many admissible frames $\mathcal F(X)$, and the choice of $Q$ becomes unstable: tiny perturbations of $X$ can cause large, discontinuous changes in the chosen eigenvectors.

This lack of uniqueness propagates to the frame averaging. The size and content of $\mathcal F(X)$ can change abruptly as the spectrum crosses a degeneracy point, making $\langle \Phi \rangle_{\mathcal F}(X)$ non-continuous as a function of $X$. For typical applications we focus on non-degenerate covariance, but in practice many datasets contain nearly planar or symmetric structures, so handling small or repeated eigenvalues is an important design consideration. Intuitively, zero or very small eigenvalues indicate that **the point cloud has low variance along some directions**, while equal eigenvalues indicate **equal variance along some directions**, which creates rotational ambiguity inside the corresponding subspace.



## Global Frame II: Miminal Frame Averaging

Minimal frame averaging seeks the simplest possible global frame by **choosing a small subset of points and orthonormalizing them**. A typical construction is to select the first three non-collinear vectors among the columns of $X$, form the $3 \times 3$ matrix with these vectors as columns, and then apply QR decomposition to obtain an orthonormal matrix $Q \in O(3)$. This provides a candidate global frame that depends only on a few points.

This approach is conceptually simple and computationally cheap, but it is **highly sensitive to the ordering and arrangement of the points**. The frame depends on which three points are chosen, so it is permutation-dependent unless we impose a specific deterministic ordering rule (for example, sorting by some scalar feature). Once the order shifts under the rule, the frame changes abruptly. Moreover, if the selected vectors become collinear or nearly collinear, QR decomposition becomes ill-conditioned or fails to produce a stable basis. 

These issues cause discontinuities in the induced mapping from $X$ to frames. As points move and cross collinearity thresholds, the frame can change sharply, which again leads to non-smooth behavior in any model that relies on this canonicalization. Frame averaging over the sign ambiguities of $Q$ can restore exact $O(3)$-equivariance, but it does not fix the fundamental instability coming from the discrete choice of which points define the frame.





## Global Frame III: Learned Canonicalization

In learned canonicalization, the frame is not determined by learnable weights that depend on the data. A common idea is to **produce three candidate vectors as weighted sums of the input points and then orthonormalize them**. Concretely, one can define three sets of scalar weights $\{\alpha_i^{(k)}(X)\}_{i=1}^n$ for $k = 1,2,3$, and then form
$$
v_k(X) = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_i^{(k)}(X)\, x_i \in \mathbb{R}^3, \qquad k=1,2,3.
$$
Stacking these as columns gives a $3 \times 3$ matrix $V(X)$, and applying **QR decomposition** yields an orthonormal frame $Q(X)$. The weights $\alpha_i^{(k)}$ are produced by a neural network, typically designed to be permutation-invariant in $i$, so that the overall canonicalization is symmetric in the input points. **Note that in this case, the number of $n$ is usually equal across samples.**

This design allows the model to learn data-dependent directions that are optimal for the downstream task, but it inherits a similar degeneracy issue: if the three vectors $v_1, v_2, v_3$ become collinear or nearly collinear, the QR procedure is unstable and the resulting frame can change abruptly with small changes in $X$ or in the learned weights. In such cases, the effective size and structure of the admissible frame set $\mathcal F(X)$ can change, undermining the continuity and smoothness of the equivariant mapping. To mitigate this, one can add regularization terms that discourage degenerate configurations, for example penalizing small pairwise angles between $v_1, v_2, v_3$ or encouraging their norms and mutual orthogonality.



## Local Frame I: Edge Frame

Local frames focus on neighborhoods, typically centered on graph edges or nodes. In the edge frame construction, we use the edge direction and cross products of associated vectors. Consider an edge $(i,j)$ in the graph with edge vector
$$
e_{ij} = x_j - x_i.
$$
We first normalize this to get a unit direction $u_1 = e_{ij}/\|e_{ij}\|$. To obtain a second direction, we select vectors $v_i$ and $v_j$ associated with the edge, for example positions $v_i = x_i$ and $v_j = x_j$, or another vectors incident to $i$ or $j$, and take a cross product:
$$
u_2 \propto v_i \times v_j.
$$
Finally, we can define a third direction as
$$
u_3 \propto u_1 \times u_2,
$$
and normalize all three vectors to get an orthonormal frame. In the language of spherical harmonics and irreducible representations, this construction can be seen as starting from $\ell=1$ vectors (the edge direction and another vector) and using their tensor product to generate new $\ell=1$ components, which are then orthonormalized.

The critical failure mode occurs **when the chosen vectors are parallel or nearly parallel**. If $v_i$ is parallel to $v_j$, their cross product vanishes, so we cannot construct a second independent direction. Even when they are only nearly parallel, $u_2$ has very small norm and is highly sensitive to noise, leading to unstable frames. As edges move or neighborhoods change, this can cause sudden flips or rotations of the local frame, which again compromises continuity.



## Local Frame II: Edge-Pair Node Frame

An alternative local construction is based on pairs of edges around a node. For a given central node $i$, we consider all unordered pairs of incident edges $(i,j)$ and $(i,k)$. Each pair defines two edge vectors
$$
e_{ij} = X_j - X_i,\qquad e_{ik} = X_k - X_i.
$$
If these vectors are not collinear, they span a local plane, and we can use Gram–Schmidt to orthonormalize them into two unit directions $u_1, u_2$. A third direction $u_3$ is then obtained as $u_3 = u_1 \times u_2$, producing an orthonormal frame for that particular edge pair. The collection of all such frames over all pairs forms a local frame set $\mathcal F_i(X)$ at node $i$.

This method systematically explores the local geometry, but it has the same core degeneracy: **when two edges are collinear, they fail to span a plane.** In such configurations, the orthonormalization becomes ill-defined, and small changes in the positions of neighboring points can cause large changes in the selected frame. One common strategy is to assign weights to each edge pair, for example based on distances, and form weighted averages over the resulting frames or their induced features. We will discuss this in the next section.



## Mitigating Discontinuity by Weighted Frame

A **weighted frame averaging** is frame averaging with **input-dependent weights** instead of a uniform average. Concretely, suppose we construct a finite set of frames
$$
\mathcal F(X) = \{g_1(X), \dots, g_K(X)\},
$$
where each $g_k(X)$ is a rotation. A weighted frame assigns **nonnegative weights**
$$
w_1(X), \dots, w_K(X) \quad\text{with}\quad \sum_{k=1}^K w_k(X) = 1,
$$
and then defines the symmetrized model as
$$
\left<\Phi\right>_{\mathcal F}(x) = \sum_{k=1}^K w_k(X)\, g_k(X)\,\big[\Phi\big(g_k(X)^{-1} X\big)\big].
$$
The important part is that both the frames $g_k(X)$ and the weights $w_k(X)$ are **constructed from $X$** in a way that respects the group action and changes smoothly when $x$ changes. And the weights $w_k(X)$ vanishes when $X$ is near degenerate. For example, to construct a smooth weight for radius graph construction, we can use combine smooth radial cutoff and smooth angular cutoff to detect graph construction changes and collinear vectors.

## Proposal: Frame via Weighted Average

For each node $i$, we construct $v_1 = \sum_j w_{ij}e_{ij}$ and normalize it to get $u_1$. We then project all edges orthogonally to $u_1$, aggregate again $v_2 = \sum_j w_{ij} \left(e_{ij} - \left(e_{ij}\cdot u_1\right)u_1\right)$, normalize to get $u_2$, and set $u_3 = u_1 \times u_2$. With smoothed weights $w_{ij}$ for degenerate cases, this gives a node-wise orthonormal frame that is **O(3)-equivariant, smooth in the coordinates, and only costs $O(\text{degree})$ per node** (no neighbor pairs, no eigen-decomposition).

Similarly, the same idea can be used for global frame. We can construct $v_1 = \sum_{i}w_i X_i$ and normalize it to get $u_1$. We then project all edges orthogonally to $u_1$, aggregate again $v_2 = \sum_j w_{i} \left(X_i - \left(X_i\cdot u_1\right)u_1\right)$, normalize to get $u_2$, and set $u_3 = u_1 \times u_2$. The frame is **smooth and only costs $O(n)$**.
